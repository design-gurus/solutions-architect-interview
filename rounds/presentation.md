# The Presentation Round

The presentation round tests whether a senior customer audience would understand a recommendation and act on it. The candidate presents a technical solution, then answers questions from interviewers who act as the audience. Reported formats differ in three ways: who chooses the topic, who listens, and how long the candidate speaks.

## Reported formats

| Company | Commonly reported format |
|---|---|
| Databricks | 1 hour on an invented customer case. The candidate asks questions first, then presents the value and a solution. This format comes from the company's own interview guide. |
| AWS, first report | One to one with the hiring manager: 20 minutes of presentation and 10 minutes of questions |
| AWS, second report | A 30-minute technical presentation on a problem the candidate solved |
| Snowflake | A presentation with a live demonstration |
| Google Cloud | A presentation of a technical solution, length not reported |

The reports disagree, so ask the recruiter for the exact length, the audience, and who chooses the topic before building any slides.

## What the round measures

- **Communication:** the audience can repeat the recommendation after the first two minutes.
- **Executive presence:** the ability to state a recommendation calmly to senior people and to keep their confidence when they challenge it.
- **Strategic thinking:** the technical choice is connected to a business goal, such as a deadline or revenue at risk.
- **Discovery:** in formats with an invented customer, the questions asked before the talk are part of the score.

## An eight-slide plan for 20 minutes

Give every slide a fixed number of minutes before writing it.

| Slide | Minutes | Content |
|---|---:|---|
| 1. Recommendation | 2 | What you propose and why, one sentence each |
| 2. The problem in numbers | 2 | The cost of doing nothing |
| 3. What discovery found | 2 | The facts that changed the design |
| 4. Architecture | 4 | One diagram, the decisions, and the rejected options |
| 5. Proof of concept | 2 | A test that shows the design works |
| 6. Migration or delivery plan | 3 | The order of the work, with dates |
| 7. Cost and risks | 3 | The estimate, its assumptions, and the top risks |
| 8. Decision and next steps | 2 | What you need from the audience |

Three rules make the plan work:

1. Write each slide title as a full sentence with a number, so the titles alone tell the story.
2. Keep three to five backup slides after the last one for detailed questions.
3. Plan 18 minutes of speech for a 20-minute slot, so that pauses and one early question still fit.

## Four types of hard question

Answer each question in about one minute: confirm it if unclear, answer in one sentence, give one piece of evidence, and check that the concern is covered.

| Type | Example | How to answer |
|---|---|---|
| Challenging | "Why not keep fixed servers and size them for the sale?" | Agree with any correct part, then name the rejected option and the evidence |
| Out of scope | "Should we also replace our finance system?" | Say that it is outside the proposal, write it down, and offer a separate time |
| Hostile | "Cloud bills always grow. Your saving is a sales number." | Answer the concern, never the tone, and offer a check in place of a promise |
| Unknown fact | "What will the vendor charge for licenses?" | Say "I do not know" first, then what you do know and a next step with a date |

## Common mistakes

- **A late recommendation.** The talk follows the project in time order, and the proposal appears at minute 15.
- **Too many slides.** Twenty-five slides in 20 minutes leave 48 seconds each.
- **A service tour.** A list of services replaces the reasons for the design.
- **Running over time.** A talk that runs 7 minutes long leaves 3 of the 10 minutes for questions.
- **The wrong depth.** Configuration details confuse executives, and business slogans frustrate engineers.
